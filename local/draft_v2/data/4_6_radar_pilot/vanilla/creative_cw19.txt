**High Seas and Low Vices: A Chapter of Lieutenant James Merrick**  

The morning air in Canton clung to the skin like a damp cloth, thick with the reek of brine, fish, and something sharper—burnt incense, maybe, or the faint tang of opium smoke. I stood at the edge of the wharf, watching the river churn beneath the hulls of merchant ships, my boots scuffed with the grit of the docks. The British barracks loomed behind me, a fortress of order and discipline, but I had no intention of returning there today. Not yet.  

“Merrick!” A voice cut through the din. I turned to see Captain Harcourt, my superior, striding toward me with the brisk efficiency of a man who’d never once been late for anything. His uniform was crisp, his posture rigid, but his eyes—those sharp, hawkish eyes—were already scanning the horizon, as if searching for something beyond the horizon.  

“Good morning, sir,” I said, forcing a smile. My teeth felt loose, a side effect of the last dose.  

“Where’s your report on the *HMS* *Cumberland*’s cargo?”  

“I—ah—thought it might be better to review the manifests first, sir. Ensure accuracy.”  

Harcourt’s brow furrowed. “You’re not the man I hired, Merrick. You’re not even the man you were last month.”  

I stiffened. “I’m fully capable, sir.”  

“Capable of what? Avoiding your duties? I’ve heard whispers about your… *absences*.” His tone was light, but the implication hung heavy.  

“I’ve been busy with… administrative matters,” I said, my voice thin.  

“Busy? Or distracted?”  

The question was a blade. I turned away, pretending to adjust my coat. “I’ll have the report ready by noon, sir.”  

He didn’t respond. Just watched me, that predator’s gaze, until I vanished into the crowd.  

---  

The market was a labyrinth of color and noise, the air thick with the scent of spices, roasted duck, and the acrid bite of tobacco. I ducked into a side alley, where the shadows pooled like ink. Here, the world was quieter, the kind of quiet that came before a storm.  

“Merrick!” A voice called, low and familiar.  

I turned to see Li Wei, a local merchant with a face like weathered leather and a smile that never reached his eyes. He gestured for me to follow, and I did, my boots crunching over broken tiles.  

“You’re looking thinner,” Li Wei said, leading me to a cramped room behind his shop. The walls were paper-thin, the air heavy with the musk of damp wood and something else—opium, I suspected.  

“I’ve been busy,” I said, my voice hoarse.  

“Busy with what?”  

“Duties. Reports. The usual.”  

He laughed, a dry, rasping sound. “You’re not fooling anyone, Merrick. You’ve been skimming the docks, trading favors, and buying your way through this city. I’ve seen the marks on your hands.”  

I clenched my fists. “I’m not a child.”  

“No, you’re a man with a habit. And habits are expensive.”  

He slid a small pouch across the table. Inside, the powder gleamed like crushed pearls. “Ten taels for this. Pay in silver, or in favors.”  

I hesitated. The pouch was a temptation, but the price was steep. “I’ll need more than that.”  

“Then you’ll need to work.”  

He leaned in, his breath warm and pungent. “There’s a shipment leaving tonight. A French vessel, bound for Macao. The captain’s a drunk, and the cargo’s… *unofficial*. You know how to handle that sort of thing.”  

I knew. I’d done it before.  

“Name your price.”  

He smiled. “A favor. And a tael.”  

I handed him the tael, then left, my mind already racing.  

---  

The warehouse was a cavern of rot and shadows, the air thick with the stench of salt and decay. The French captain, a man with a face like a broken clock, slumped over a barrel of rum, his eyes glazed.  

“Merrick,” he mumbled, not looking up. “You’re late.”  

“I had a… delay,” I said, my voice flat.  

He snorted. “You’re not the only one with a delay. The cargo’s here. You’ll take it to Macao. No questions. No problems.”  

I nodded, my hands trembling. The box was heavy, its contents wrapped in oilcloth. I didn’t need to open it to know what was inside.  

As I left, the captain muttered, “You’ll be sorry, Merrick. You always are.”  

---  

The opium den was a den of whispers and smoke, the kind of place where time folded in on itself. The room was dim, lit only by the flicker of oil lamps and the glow of a dozen faces, each one a mask of hunger.  

“Merrick!” A voice called.  

I turned to see Old Chen, a dealer with a voice like gravel and a smile that never reached his eyes. He gestured to a chair, and I sat, my back to the wall, the weight of the box pressing against my ribs.  

“You’re late,” Old Chen said, sliding a small vial across the table.  

“I had… business.”  

He chuckled. “You always have business. But you’re not the only one with a habit. The captain’s been asking about you.”  

I stiffened. “He’s not my problem.”  

“He’s your problem. And he’s not the only one. You’re running out of time, Merrick. The navy’s watching. And the *dragon* is hungry.”  

I took the vial, my fingers shaking. The powder inside was a promise, a curse, a lifeline.  

“Time’s running out,” Old Chen said, his voice a whisper. “You’ll be sorry.”  

I didn’t answer. I just inhaled, the smoke curling into my lungs, and let the world blur.  

---  

By the time the sun dipped below the horizon, the city was a haze of lanterns and shadows. I wandered the docks, my mind a fog, the weight of the box a phantom on my back. The sea whispered promises, but I had no ship to sail. Only the dragon, and the price of its breath.  

I’d survive another day. I always did.
